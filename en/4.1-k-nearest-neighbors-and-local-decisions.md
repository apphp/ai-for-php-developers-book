# 4.1 k-Nearest Neighbors and Local Decisions

The k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) algorithm is one of the most intuitive and fundamental machine learning algorithms. It makes almost no assumptions about the data, does not train a parametric model, and instead stores the training data and relies on a simple, almost geometric idea: _similar objects should have similar outcomes_.

This makes k-NN especially useful for understanding what local decisions are, why data geometry matters, and how the choice of distance metric directly affects the result.

#### Local Decisions Instead of a Global Model

Unlike linear or logistic regression, k-NN does not try to find one general formula for all the data. It has no coefficients, weights, or explicitly optimized parametric loss function.

The algorithm works differently:

1. We store all the training data.
2. For a new object, we find the _k_ nearest examples.
3. We make a decision based only on these neighbors.

This is a local decision. For each new point, the decision is built again based on its immediate neighborhood.

You can think of k-NN as answering the question:

> What usually happens to objects _similar to this particular one_?

#### Geometric Intuition of k-NN

Consider the simplest case – a space with two features. Each object is a point on a plane. The algorithm then works in a purely geometric way:

* there is a query point
* we measure the distances to all other points
* we select the kk points with the smallest distance

For classification, the most common approach is voting (`mode` in statistics is the value that occurs most often in a dataset):

$$
\hat{y} = \operatorname{mode}(y_1, y_2, \dots, y_k)\
$$

For regression, we use the average:

$$
\hat{y} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} y_i
$$

So, the decision is determined by the shape of the local "cloud" of points around the query.

#### Distance Metrics: How We Define "Closeness"

The key question in k-NN is: what does "nearest" mean? The answer is defined by the distance metric.

**Euclidean Distance**

Euclidean distance is the most common metric:

$$
d(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}
$$

It works well when:

* features have similar scales
* the space has relatively low dimensionality (see the curse of dimensionality below)
* the geometric shape of the data clouds is important

**Manhattan Distance**

Manhattan distance is useful when the straight-line distance is less important than the sum of movements along the axes:

$$
d(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|
$$

This metric is often used when the contribution of each feature to the distance is additive and can be interpreted as the "cost" of each step.

**Minkowski Distance**

The generalized form is the Minkowski distance:

$$
d(x, y) = \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i - y_i|^p \right)^{1/p}
$$

* when $$p = 1$$ – we get Manhattan distance
* when $$p = 2$$ – we get Euclidean distance

The choice of _p_ allows us to smoothly change the shape of the point's "neighborhood".

**Cosine Distance**

Sometimes the direction of a vector matters more than its length. Formally, this is cosine similarity, not a distance.

$$
\cos(\theta) = \frac{x \cdot y}{||x|| \ ||y||}
$$

In practice, k-NN often uses cosine distance:

$$
d(x, y) = 1 - \cos(\theta)
$$

Strictly speaking, this function is not a metric in the mathematical sense, but it is widely used in practice. For example, it is useful for text, embeddings, and recommendation tasks where absolute values matter less than relative proportions.

#### Choosing k: The Bias–Variance Tradeoff

The parameter kk determines how local the decision will be.

Small _k_ (for example, $$k = 1$$):

* very sensitive to noise
* low bias, high variance

Large _k_:

* smoother decisions
* higher bias, lower variance

k-NN is a clear example of the classic bias–variance tradeoff.

#### Dimensionality and the Curse of Dimensionality

As the number of features increases, we encounter a problem known as the curse of dimensionality.

Intuitively, this can be explained as follows:

* in high-dimensional spaces, all points become "far away"
* the difference between the nearest and farthest neighbor becomes smaller
* distance metrics become less informative

For many distributions in high-dimensional spaces, we observe an effect where the ratio:

$$
\frac{d\_{\min}}{d\_{\max}} \to 1
$$

This makes k-NN less effective without:

* feature normalization
* feature selection
* dimensionality reduction, for example, using PCA or autoencoders

#### Why k-NN Is Conceptually Important

Despite its simplicity and computational cost when performing a direct search on large datasets, k-NN remains an important algorithm for understanding machine learning:

* it shows the difference between local and global models
* it makes data geometry visible
* it highlights the role of metrics and scales
* it demonstrates the bias–variance tradeoff without complex mathematics

k-NN is an almost "honest" algorithm. It does not hide its logic behind weights and layers. Instead, it simply says: _look at your neighbors_.

That is why it is so useful for developing intuition, even though it is not used as often in production code.
