# 4.2 Decision Trees and space partitioning

## 4.2 Decision Trees and Feature Space Splitting

Entropy, information gain, and interpretability.

Decision trees are one of the most intuitive yet powerful machine learning algorithms. They are popular because they are easy to visualize, closely follow human "if–then" reasoning, and produce results that can be explained not only to engineers but also to business stakeholders, managers, and even customers. Unlike many other models, a decision tree does not look like a black box – you can literally draw it on paper.

In this chapter, we will explore how a decision tree progressively splits the feature space, why this process relies on impurity measures such as entropy and the Gini index, what information gain means, and why these ideas make decision trees so interpretable.

#### A Tree as a Sequence of Questions

At its simplest, a decision tree is a set of questions. Each internal node asks a question about one of the features: "x > 5?", "color = red?", or "age < 30?". Depending on the answer, the object follows one of the branches. The leaves contain the final decision – a class or a numerical value.

From a geometric perspective, the tree splits the feature space step by step, dividing it into regions. The initial splits are broad, and subsequent ones become more precise. Each new question narrows down the part of the space in which the object lies.

It is important to understand that a tree does not search for the perfect solution all at once. It uses a "greedy" approach: at each step, it chooses the question that best organizes the data at that moment. In other words, it does not look several steps ahead when making its choice.

#### Splitting the Feature Space

Consider a two-dimensional case with features $$x_1$$ and $$x_2$$. A decision tree draws vertical or horizontal lines parallel to the feature axes (in classical trees such as CART; generalized trees with oblique splits also exist). These lines divide the plane into rectangular regions. Within each region, the predictions become more uniform.

For example, the first split might be the line $$x_1 < a_2$$. One of the resulting halves might then be split by the line $$x_2 < b_1$$, and so on. Gradually, the feature space becomes a mosaic of rectangles.

This is a key difference between decision trees and linear models. Linear regression and logistic regression use a single linear boundary in the original feature space. A tree creates a piecewise, step-like boundary that can approximate even very complex shapes.

#### Node Purity and the Intuition Behind Entropy

To decide which question to ask at the next node, we need a formal way to define a "good split". Intuitively, a good split is one that makes the data in the child nodes more homogeneous in terms of class membership.

If all objects in a node belong to the same class, no further question is needed – this is the ideal case. If the classes are mixed, the node is "impure".

This idea is formalized by entropy. In binary classification, it is defined as follows:

$$
H(p) = -p \ \log_2(p) - (1 - p)\log_2(1 - p)
$$

where $$p$$ is the proportion of objects belonging to the first class.

If $$p = 0$$ or $$p = 1$$, entropy is $$0$$ – there is no uncertainty. If $$p = 0.5$$, entropy reaches its maximum – we cannot confidently predict which class an object belongs to.

Entropy measures uncertainty. The higher it is, the harder it is to make a decision.

#### Information Gain – A Criterion for Choosing Questions

We now have a way to evaluate the quality of a split. Suppose the current node has entropy \$$H\_{parent}\$$. We try asking a question and obtain several child nodes. We can calculate their weighted average entropy:

$$
H_{\text{children}} = \sum_i \frac{N_i}{N} \ H_i
$$

where $$N_i$$ is the number of objects in the (i)-th child node.

Information gain is defined as the reduction in entropy:

$$
IG = H_{parent} - H_{children}
$$

The greater the information gain, the better the question. It reduces uncertainty more effectively and makes the data more "pure".

Importantly, at each step, the tree chooses the question with the highest information gain for the current node. Optimization is local at every step – the tree does not revisit splits it has already made.

#### A Small Numerical Example

Suppose a node contains 10 objects: 6 from class A and 4 from class B. The entropy of this node is approximately 0.97.

We try splitting on the feature $$x < 5$$ and obtain two nodes:

* The first contains 4 objects from class A and none from class B, so its entropy is 0.
* The second contains 2 objects from class A and 4 from class B, so its entropy is approximately 0.92.

Total number of objects: 10

Split:

* Node 1: 4 objects (all A), ( $$H_1 = 0$$ )
* Node 2: 6 objects (2 A, 4 B), ( $$H_2 \approx 0.92$$ )

Weighted entropy formula:\
\[ $$H_{split} = \frac{n_1}{N} H_1 + \frac{n_2}{N} H_2$$ ]

Substituting the values:\
\[ $$H_{split} = \frac{4}{10} \cdot 0 + \frac{6}{10} \cdot 0.92$$ ]\
\[ $$H_{split} = 0 + 0.6 \cdot 0.92 = 0.552$$ ]

**Answer: approximately 0.55**

The information gain is then: \[ $$IG = 0.97 - 0.55 \approx 0.42$$ ]

This is a fairly good gain – the split is genuinely useful.

The weighted entropy is lower than the original entropy, and the difference is the information gain. Decision trees prefer splits that achieve this reduction.

#### Greediness, Overfitting, and Tree Depth

Because of their "greedy" nature, decision trees can easily overfit. If a tree is allowed to grow without restrictions, it may memorize the training data. This is related to high model variance and low bias, and can result in leaves containing only one or two objects.

In extreme cases, this can lead to zero training error but poor generalization. That is why practical implementations almost always impose constraints, such as maximum depth, minimum number of samples per node, and minimum information gain.

These parameters directly affect the shape of the feature space partitioning – from large, coarse regions to a fine-grained mosaic.

#### Interpretability of Decision Trees

One of the main advantages of decision trees is their interpretability. Any decision can be represented as a path from the root to a leaf:

"If age < 30, income > X, and region = Europe, then class = A."

This explanation is easy for people to understand. It can be included in a report, logs, or a product interface. That is why decision trees are often used in fintech, medicine, and other fields where explaining a model's decisions is important.

Trees also allow us to estimate feature importance. In practice, feature importance is usually calculated as the total reduction in impurity across the tree. This often correlates with how frequently a feature is used and how high up in the tree it appears. However, these estimates can be biased, for example, in favor of features with many unique values or correlated features. This idea is later extended by ensemble methods such as Random Forest and Gradient Boosting.

#### When Decision Trees Are Particularly Useful

The interpretability of decision trees largely determines where they are most useful.

They are especially suitable for tasks where a decision must not only be made but also explained, such as credit scoring, fraud detection, content moderation, application filtering, and risk assessment. In these systems, it is important not only to predict an outcome but also to explain why it was predicted.

In many such cases, a model effectively replaces a set of business rules. Decision trees do this naturally through a sequence of simple conditions: "if X > threshold, then…"

Trees also work well when the data can be interpreted through thresholds and logical splits rather than complex continuous relationships.

However, there are types of problems for which a single decision tree is less suitable:

* When maximum accuracy is required – a single tree usually performs worse than an ensemble.
* When the relationships between features are smooth and complex.
* When a stable approximation without abrupt splits is required.

#### Geometric Limitations of Decision Trees

Despite their flexibility, decision trees have limitations. Their splits are axis-aligned – parallel to the feature axes. This means they approximate slanted or diagonal boundaries using a series of step-like splits.

For complex problems, a single tree often turns out to be either too coarse or too overfitted. That is why real-world systems frequently use trees in ensembles – methods that combine multiple trees into a single model to improve accuracy and stability.

#### Summary

A decision tree is an algorithm that progressively reduces uncertainty by splitting the feature space into increasingly homogeneous regions. Entropy measures uncertainty, information gain provides a criterion for choosing questions, and the tree's structure makes the model interpretable and easy to understand.

Understanding these ideas is important not only for working with decision trees but also for understanding ensemble methods that build on them and strengthen their advantages.
